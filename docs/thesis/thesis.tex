\section{Theoretical Background}

This chapter introduced the theoretical foundations required for this thesis. It covered the pinhole camera model, including intrinsic and extrinsic parameters as well as lens distortion, as the basis for geometric camera modeling. Building on this, common camera calibration methods and error metrics were presented, including target-based calibration and bundle adjustment. The chapter also introduced light field camera systems, their main system types, and the state of the art in their calibration. Together, these concepts provide the necessary background for the calibration approach and experiments presented in the following chapters.

% Chapter 2.1 Pinhole Camera Model
\subsection{Pinhole Camera Model}

Cameras are the primary devices used to capture visual information from the real world into an image. To process and analyze this information computationally, it is necessary to describe the imaging process using a  camera model \cite{StanfordCS231ACameraModels}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/Pinhole-Camera.png}
    \caption{Illustration of the pinhole camera model. Source: \cite{MathWorksCameraCalibration}.}
    \label{fig:pinhole_camera_model}
\end{figure}

The pinhole camera model describes a simple imaging system in which light rays
from a 3D scene pass through a small aperture and are projected through the focal point onto a 2D image plane \cite{StanfordCS231ACameraModels}. The aperture limits the incoming light such that each point in the scene is mapped to a single point on the image plane. In this model, the projected image is inverted due to the geometry of the projection, as shown in \autoref{fig:pinhole_camera_model}.
\vspace{0.2cm}

To define the mapping from 3D world coordinates to 2D image coordinates,
the model is mathematically described using two types of parameters \cite{MathWorksCameraCalibration}.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Intrinsic Parameters: \hspace{6mm}} 
  \[
  K = 
  \begin{bmatrix}
  f_x & s & c_x \\
  0 & f_y & c_y \\
  0 & 0 & 1
  \end{bmatrix}
  \] 
  
  \vspace{0.1cm}
  where $(f_x, f_y)$ are the focal lengths in pixel units along the x and y axes,
  $s$ is the skew coefficient between the axes, and $(c_x, c_y)$ is the principal point (optical center) in pixel coordinates.
  $K$ is also known as the camera matrix.

  \item \textbf{Extrinsic Parameters:} 
  \[
  [R \mid t] = 
  \begin{bmatrix}
  r_{11} & r_{12} & r_{13} & t_x \\
  r_{21} & r_{22} & r_{23} & t_y \\
  r_{31} & r_{32} & r_{33} & t_z
  \end{bmatrix}
  \]

  where $R$ is a $3 \times 3$ rotation matrix representing the orientation of the camera with respect to the world coordinate system, and $t$ is a $3 \times 1$ translation vector representing the position of the camera center in world coordinates.
\end{itemize}

These parameters form the basis of camera calibration, which aims to estimate them accurately. However in practice, cameras use lenses to focus light onto the image sensor, which introduces distortions not accounted for in the idealized pinhole model.

% Chapter 2.2 Lens Distortion
\subsection{Lens Distortion}

The camera matrix alone is not sufficient to model real imaging systems, as the
ideal pinhole camera model assumes a lens-free projection. In practice, however, camera lenses introduce various optical distortions that must be taken into account to accurately describe the image formation process \cite{MathWorksCameraCalibration}.
\newline

Lens distortion is defined as an optical aberration that causes straight lines in the real world to appear curved in the captured image \cite{ScienceDirectLensDistortion}. Since distortion alters the geometric relationship between 3D world points and their 2D image projections, it has a direct impact on the accuracy of camera calibration.
If lens distortion is not properly modeled, the estimated intrinsic and extrinsic parameters can become biased, leading to unreliable calibration results \cite{ricolfe2010lens}.
\newline

In most camera calibration models, lens distortion is represented using a small
set of parametric functions. The two most common types of distortion considered
in practice are radial distortion and tangential distortion.


% Chapter 2.2.1 Radial Distortion
\subsubsection{Radial Distortion}

Radial distortion is caused by the spherical shape of camera lenses, which leads
to different refraction of light from the image center to the outer regions.
This causes straight lines in the image to appear curved, resulting in barrel
or pincushion distortion \cite{RadialDistortion}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/Radial-Distortion.png}
    \caption{Comparison of different types of radial distortion. Source: \cite{opencv_calib3d_docs}.}
    \label{fig:radial_distortion}
\end{figure}

The formal definition of radial distortion is given as follows \cite{MathWorksCameraCalibration}:

\begin{align}
x_{distorted} = x \left( 1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6 \right) \label{eq:1}
\end{align}

\begin{align}
y_{distorted} = y \left( 1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6 \right) \label{eq:2}
\end{align}

\vspace{0.2mm}
where $x$ and $y$ denote the undistorted normalized image coordinates in the camera coordinate system. The radius $r$ represents the distance of a point from the image center and is defined as:

\begin{align}
r^2 = x^2 + y^2 \label{eq:3}
\end{align}

The coefficients $k_1$, $k_2$, and $k_3$ in \eqref{eq:1} and \eqref{eq:2} control the strength of radial lens distortion. In practice, the first coefficient $k_1$ has the dominant influence on the distortion type: negative values of $k_1$ typically result in barrel distortion, while positive values lead to pincushion distortion.


% Chapter 2.2.2 Tangential Distortion
\subsubsection{Tangential Distortion}

Tangential distortion occurs when the image sensor and the camera lens
are not perfectly aligned or parallel to each other \cite{MathWorksCameraCalibration}. As a result, image points are displaced tangentially with respect to the optical axis. \autoref{fig:tangential_distortion} illustrates the geometric cause of tangential distortion and its effect on the captured image.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/Tangential-Distortion.png}
    \hspace{1cm}
    \includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{figures/Tangential-Distortion2.png}
    \caption{Visualization of tangential distortion. Source: \cite{MathWorksCameraCalibration}, \cite{Lin2024Stereo}.}
    \label{fig:tangential_distortion}
\end{figure}

The formal definition of tangential distortion is given by the following equations \cite{MathWorksCameraCalibration}:

\begin{align}
x_{\text{distorted}} = x + \left( 2 p_1 x y + p_2 \left( r^2 + 2x^2 \right) \right)
\label{eq:tangential_x}
\end{align}

\begin{align}
y_{\text{distorted}} = y + \left( p_1 \left( r^2 + 2y^2 \right) + 2 p_2 x y \right)
\label{eq:tangential_y}
\end{align}

\vspace{0.2cm}
Here, $x$ and $y$ denote the undistorted normalized image coordinates in the camera coordinate system. The radius $r$ represents the distance of a point from the image center and is defined as:

\begin{align}
r^2 = x^2 + y^2
\label{eq:tangential_r}
\end{align}

The coefficients $p_1$ and $p_2$ describe how strong the tangential lens distortion is. This type of distortion is caused by mechanical misalignment of the lens elements relative to the image sensor, resulting in a displacement of image points. Although tangential distortion is typically less pronounced than radial distortion, it can still negatively affect the camera calibration accuracy if not properly modeled.

% 2.3 Camera Calibration
\subsection{Camera Calibration}

The goal of camera calibration, also known as camera resectioning, is to estimate the parameters of a camera model. These parameters include the intrinsic parameters, the extrinsic parameters, and the lens distortion coefficients \cite{MathWorksCameraCalibration}. Knowing these parameters makes it possible to correct distortions in the recorded images and to perform metric measurements in the scene, for example to estimate object sizes or distances from image data.
\newline

Camera calibration is required for many tasks in 3D computer vision and photometry. Applications such as depth estimation, 3D reconstruction, or pose estimation rely on a precise mapping from 3D world coordinates to 2D image coordinates \cite{song2025camera}. If the calibration is inaccurate, errors can propagate through the processing pipeline and reduce the quality of the final results.
\newline

In practice, cameras are often calibrated using targets with known geometric properties. Planar targets, such as checkerboard patterns, are widely used because they are easy to handle and lead to reliable results \cite{888718}. Several images of the target are captured from different viewpoints and orientations. Based on these images, the camera parameters can be estimated with relatively small calibration errors. For this reason, target-based calibration methods are commonly used in many computer vision applications.

% 2.3.1 Target-Based Camera Calibration
\subsubsection{Target-Based Camera Calibration}

There are various approaches for target-based camera calibration. One of the most common methods uses a checkerboard pattern, as mentioned before. The checkerboard consists of a regular grid of black and white squares with known dimensions, as shown in \autoref{fig:checkerboard}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.26\textwidth]{figures/checkerboard.png}
    \caption{Example of a checkerboard and detected corner points. Source: \cite{HedleyJonesCalibrationCheckerboards}.}
    \label{fig:checkerboard}
\end{figure}

During the calibration process, control points are extracted from the images. In the case of a checkerboard, these control points correspond to the corner points of the squares \cite{song2025camera}. The positions of these corners are known in the coordinate system of the calibration target. By capturing multiple images of the checkerboard from different angles and distances, a set of 2D image points can be obtained that correspond to the known 3D points of the target. Based on these point correspondences, an optimization problem can be formulated to estimate the intrinsic and extrinsic camera parameters as well as the lens distortion coefficients \cite{888718}.
\newline

This calibration approach is implemented in widely used computer vision libraries such as OpenCV \cite{OpenCV_Calibration}. It can be applied to
single-camera setups and multi-camera systems. Besides checkerboards, other types of calibration targets exist, for example circular dot patterns \cite{song2025camera}. In some cases, these patterns can achieve higher accuracy, especially when the image quality is low. However, they also require physical targets and a manual calibration setup.
\newline

The main limitations of target-based calibration methods are the required manual placement of the target and the need to capture images with different poses. In addition, the calibration accuracy depends on factors such as the quality of the corner detection, the number of images used, and the diversity of viewpoints during data acquisition \cite{888718}. Despite these limitations, target-based calibration remains a standard method in many computer vision applications due to its simplicity and reliable performance.

% 2.3.2 Calibration Parameters and Error Metrics
\subsubsection{Calibration Parameters and Error Metrics}

To evaluate the quality of a camera calibration, different error metrics are used. These metrics describe how well the estimated camera parameters fit the observed image data. In practice, this is done by comparing detected image points with points that are projected using the calibrated camera model. The most common metric is the reprojection error \cite{MathWorksCameraCalibration}.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/reprojection_error.png}
    \caption{Visualization of reprojection error. Source: \cite{CamCalibReprojectionError}.}
    \label{fig:reprojection_error}
\end{figure}

After a calibration, known 3D points are projected back onto the image plane. The reprojection error measures the distance between the observed image points and the corresponding projected points. It is usually computed as the average Euclidean distance and is given in pixel units \cite{CamCalibReprojectionError}. It can be defined as

\begin{align}
e = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \| \mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{q}_i \|_2 .
\label{eq:7}
\end{align}

Here, $\mathbf{p}_i$ represents the observed feature points in the image, while $\mathbf{q}_i$ denotes the projected points obtained from the calibrated camera model. In general, a smaller reprojection error indicates a better calibration. What is considered a good value depends on the application, but for accurate calibrations the reprojection error is often between 0.1 and 0.5 pixels \cite{Huang2016Camera}.
\newline

Another commonly used measure is the root mean square (RMS) error \cite{Hartley2003}. It summarizes the reprojection error over all calibration points and is computed as:

\begin{align} \epsilon_{\mathrm{res}} = \left( \frac{1}{2n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big\| \mathbf{p}'_i - \mathbf{q}'_i \big\|_2^2 \right)^{1/2} \label{eq:8} \end{align}

The RMS error provides a single value that describes the overall calibration accuracy and is often used to compare different calibration results.

% 2.3.3 Bundle Adjustment
\subsubsection{Bundle Adjustment}

Bundle adjustment is a method for improving a 3D reconstruction by refining both the positions of 3D points and the camera parameters at the same time \cite{Triggs2000BundleAdjustment}. The name comes from the “bundles” of light rays from the 3D points to the cameras, which are adjusted together to make the reconstruction more accurate. The parameters that are optimized together usually include the 3D coordinates of the points, the extrinsic parameters, and sometimes the intrinsic parameters \cite{cremers2019bundleadjustment}.
\newline

This joint optimization improves calibration accuracy because it ensures that all cameras and points are consistent with each other. As a result, projection errors are reduced and small misalignments are corrected that would remain if cameras or points were adjusted separately \cite{Triggs2000BundleAdjustment}. This idea is illustrated in \autoref{fig:bundle_adjustment}.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{figures/bundle_adjustement.png}
    \caption{Visual representation of bundle adjustment. Source: \cite{Baeldung2024BundleAdjustment}.}
    \label{fig:bundle_adjustment}
\end{figure}

Mathematically, bundle adjustment is formulated as an optimization problem that minimizes the reprojection error. This optimization is usually solved using nonlinear least squares methods and takes advantage of the fact that the problem structure is largely sparse \cite{cremers2019bundleadjustment}.

% 2.4 Light Field Cameras Systems
\subsection{Light Field Camera Systems}

A standard camera captures a 2D projection of the 3D world. Light field cameras, on the other hand, have the ability to capture more information. They record not only the intensity of light rays but also their direction \cite{DLR2026LightFieldCameras}. This property allows the creation of accurate depth maps and refocus the image after it has been taken \cite{LiFCal}. Therefore light field cameras are growing in importance for applications in computer vision, robotics, augmented reality, and other applications.
\newline

The four-dimensional nature of light fields means that their processing currently needs relatively high computational resources \cite{DLR2026LightFieldCameras}. However, the evolution of hardware and algorithms is making light field cameras more practical for real-world applications. 
\newline

There are mainly two different types of light field camera systems: multi-camera arrays and micro-lens-array-based cameras. Each type has its own advantages and disadvantages, which will be discussed in the following sections.

% 2.4.1 Multi-Camera Light Field Systems
\subsubsection{Multi-Camera Light Field Systems}
% Camera arrays (e.g. cross-shaped setup with 13 cameras)

Multi-camera arrays are one way to capture light fields. This type of system is structured by arranging multiple individual cameras in a geometric configuration \cite{multi_camera_calibration}. Each camera captures a different viewpoint of the scene, enabling the system to record a 4D light field. In practice, there are different approaches to arranging the cameras. For example, in \cite{StanfordArrayProject}, an array of 128 cameras in a square grid is used, while in other works cross-shaped structures with fewer cameras are employed \cite{multi_camera_calibration}, as shown in \autoref{fig:multi_camera_array}.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/multi_camera_array.png}
    \caption{Representation of a multi-camera system. Source: \cite{multi_camera_calibration}.}
    \label{fig:multi_camera_array}
\end{figure}

Besides the advantages of a multi-camera setup, such as improved depth accuracy and refocusing capabilities, there are also challenges. The calibration of such systems is more complex and requires higher computational effort due to the larger number of cameras involved \cite{multi_camera_calibration}.
\newline

It is also essential to keep the cameras properly aligned and synchronized. Even small errors in alignment or timing can cause artifacts or undesired results in the final image. Therefore, multi-camera light field systems require a very precise setup and calibration to work as intended.

% 2.4.2 Micro-Lens-Array-Based Light Field Cameras
\subsubsection{Micro-Lens-Array-Based Light Field Cameras}
% MLA-based systems as used in LiFCal

The second type of light field camera is based on a micro-lens array (MLA). In this case instead of having different cameras, the system uses only one sensor combined with an array of small lenses placed between the main lens and the image sensor \cite{LiFCal}. The MLA allows the camera to capture also the 4D light field information. 

\vspace{0.2cm}
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/MLA.png}
    \caption{Structure of a micro-lens-array-based light field camera. Source: \cite{LiFCal}.}
    \label{fig:mla}
\end{figure}

As illustrated in \autoref{fig:mla}, each lens in the MLA focuses light from different directions onto different regions of the sensor. This way, the camera is also able to capture the same information as a multi-camera array, but with a more compact design. Because of the smaller size and lower cost, MLA-based light field cameras are mostly used in consumer applications \cite{LeeJi2017LightfieldReconstruction}. However, microlens-array cameras have to compromise between spatial and angular resolution, while multi-camera systems capture full-resolution images with sharper results and more accurate depth information. 
\newline

For MLA-based light field cameras, the pattern-based calibration method provides satisfactory results in terms of reprojection error. In practice, the overall calibration quality strongly depends on the calibration target, particularly its flatness \cite{anisimov2021calibration_auto_refinement}.

% 2.5 State of the Art in Light Field Camera Calibration
\subsection{State of the Art in Light Field Camera Calibration}

Accurate calibration is a central requirement for light field camera systems, as even small geometric errors directly affect depth estimation and view consistency. In camera arrays, where many individual views must agree, calibration errors accumulate and lead to visible artifacts in depth maps and reconstructed images.
\newline

Early work on light field calibration mainly relies on pattern-based approaches. Dima et al.\ systematically evaluate several multi-camera calibration methods using ground truth data and show that calibration accuracy strongly depends on the array geometry and the physical setup of the calibration target \cite{7548887}. In particular, small deviations such as bending or uneven surfaces of the calibration board already lead to increased reprojection errors across the array. Yang et al.\ demonstrate that using a simple checkerboard target can still yield stable calibration results for camera arrays while reducing setup complexity \cite{9172948}. However, the accuracy of these methods remains closely tied to target quality and careful placement.
\newline

To overcome the limitations of target-based calibration, several self-calibration approaches have been proposed. Zhang et al.\ introduce a fixed relative pose prior to stabilize self-calibration in camera arrays \cite{10649654}. By constraining relative camera poses, their method reduces pose drift in situations where image features are sparse or unevenly distributed. Zhou et al.\ address low-texture sports scenes and use temporal fusion across multiple frames to estimate calibration parameters \cite{9998530}. These approaches reduce the need for calibration targets, but still depend on sufficient scene structure and a reasonable initial alignment.
\newline

Some methods focus on specific system layouts or downstream tasks. Ge et al.\ study a ring-shaped multi-camera system and use a transparent calibration target to support panoramic measurements \cite{9923591}. Xie et al.\ show that accurate camera array calibration is essential for reliable depth map fusion, as calibration errors propagate directly into depth artifacts, especially near object boundaries \cite{10456113}.
\newline

The approach most relevant to this thesis is LiFCal by Fleith et al.\ \cite{LiFCal}. Instead of using a physical calibration target, LiFCal moves the MLA-based camera through the scene and estimates calibration parameters via bundle adjustment driven by depth maps from multiple viewpoints. This shifts the calibration problem from controlled target acquisition to scene structure and camera motion, making the method particularly suitable for scenarios where calibration targets are impractical or unavailable.
\newline

Overall, existing work shows that calibration accuracy in light field systems remains sensitive to setup quality, motion, and scene structure. Larger camera arrays amplify small errors, and target-free methods require reliable depth estimates and sufficient camera motion to properly constrain the geometry.

\section{Methods and Materials}

This chapter introduces the methods and materials used in this thesis, covering both the design of the calibration framework and the technical setup used for its implementation and evaluation.

\subsection{Methods}

In this first section, the design and implementation of the calibration software are described. It describes the overall workflow, the use case scenario, and the specific requirements that guided the development process. The UML class diagram and state diagram illustrate the software architecture and behavior.

\subsubsection{Overall approach}

The goal of this thesis is to develop and evaluate an automatic data-driven calibration framework for multi-camera light field systems based on the target-free calibration method proposed by Fleith et al.~\cite{LiFCal}. As discussed in the state of the art, classical target-based calibration approaches require manual intervention and careful placement of calibration targets, which limits their applicability in long-term deployments or scenarios with restricted human access.
\newline

The proposed approach uses a reliable target-based calibration as an initial reference and ground truth. This initial calibration is performed using a checkerboard pattern and serves as a baseline for evaluating subsequent target-free recalibration steps. After this initial setup, the system captures a small set of images from the scene and applies LiFCal to automatically recalibrate the multi-camera system. To assess whether the target-free recalibration is acceptable, a health monitoring mechanism is introduced. This mechanism compares the calibration results obtained from LiFCal with the baseline calibration using defined decision metrics. If the recalibration achieves a calibration quality comparable to or better than the initial calibration, it is accepted and replaces the previous baseline. Otherwise, the system retains the existing calibration parameters.
\newline

This workflow is designed to enable periodic self-recalibration without manual intervention. A potential application scenario is a robotic system deployed in a remote or inaccessible environment, where mechanical shifts or environmental changes may degrade calibration quality over time. By regularly capturing image sets and applying the proposed recalibration process, the system can maintain accurate camera parameters throughout its operation.
\newline

To clearly describe the interaction between the individual components and calibration phases, a use case description is introduced in the following section.

\subsubsection{Use Case Description}

The following use case describes the intended workflow of the proposed automatic calibration framework for a cross-shaped multi-camera light field system consisting of a central camera and twelve surrounding cameras, arranged in four cardinal directions (North, East, South, and West). The workflow is structured into three consecutive phases: an initial reference calibration, periodic target-free recalibration, and a health-based decision step.

\vspace{0.3cm}

\paragraph{Phase 1 — Initial Reference Calibration (once)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Target-based calibration (OpenCV):}
  A one-time checkerboard-based calibration is performed to estimate the intrinsic
  and extrinsic parameters for all cameras of the cross-shaped array.

  \item \textbf{Optional refinement:}
  An optional global refinement using bundle adjustment is applied to improve
  consistency across all camera parameters.

  \item \textbf{Baseline creation:}
  A global health score is computed for the initial calibration and stored as
  \texttt{baseline.json}. This baseline represents the known-good reference
  calibration for subsequent recalibration decisions.

  \item \textbf{Logging:}
  The complete initial calibration is stored as a timestamped JSON file and a
  summary entry is appended to \texttt{calibration.log}.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}

\paragraph{Phase 2 — Automated Recalibration Attempts (periodic scheduler)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Trigger:}
  An automated recalibration cycle is executed at fixed time intervals
  (e.g.\ every five minutes).

  \item \textbf{Conditional data preparation:}
  If LiFCal-compatible data (focus and depth directories) already exists and is
  complete, it is reused. Otherwise, the system generates the required inputs by
  running \texttt{run\_imageset\_creation.py}.

  \item \textbf{LiFCal recalibration run:}
  The script \texttt{run\_LiFCal\_calibration.py} executes LiFCal-based
  recalibration for all cameras in the array and produces per-camera parameter
  files as well as a combined multi-camera result.

  \item \textbf{Logging and traceability:}
  The combined recalibration result is stored as a timestamped JSON file and all
  relevant execution details are appended to \texttt{calibration.log}.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}

\paragraph{Phase 3 — Health Evaluation and Baseline Update Rule}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Health score computation:}
  A single global health score in the range $[0,100]$ is computed from the combined
  LiFCal calibration result.

  \item \textbf{Comparison against baseline:}
  The newly computed health score is compared to the score stored in the current
  baseline calibration.

  \item \textbf{Acceptance rule:}
  If the new health score is strictly higher than the baseline score, the baseline
  calibration is replaced by the new result. Otherwise, the existing baseline
  remains unchanged.

  \item \textbf{Continuous improvement loop:}
  The recalibration process is repeated periodically, allowing improved calibration
  results to update the baseline over time without manual intervention.
\end{itemize}

This structure allows the system to maintain accurate calibration parameters over time, adapting to changes in the environment or camera setup. The health-based decision mechanism ensures that only beneficial recalibrations are accepted, preserving the integrity of the calibration throughout the system's operation.
\newline

This use case serves as the conceptual basis for the requirements analysis and system design presented in the following sections.

\subsubsection{Requirements Analysis}

The requirements for the proposed automatic calibration framework are divided into functional and non-functional requirements. These requirements define the necessary features and qualities of the system to achieve the intended use case. 

\paragraph{Functional Requirements}

\begin{itemize}

  \item \textbf{FR1 – Initial Reference Calibration (Target-based):} \\
  The system shall perform a one-time, checkerboard-based initial calibration using OpenCV
  to estimate intrinsic and extrinsic parameters for the cross-shaped camera array.
  This initial calibration shall be stored as the baseline calibration and serve as
  the reference state for all subsequent health evaluations and recalibration decisions.

  \item \textbf{FR2 – Parameter Export and Standardized Representation:} \\
  The system shall export all calibration results (initial calibration and LiFCal-based recalibration)
  in a standardized, machine-readable JSON format, including intrinsic parameters,
  extrinsic poses, reprojection error statistics, timestamps, and metadata.

  \item \textbf{FR3 – Periodic Calibration Health Monitoring:} \\
  The system shall periodically (at least once per configured scheduler interval) evaluate the calibration quality by computing a global health score.

  \item \textbf{FR4 – Health Score Computation (Global Scalar Metric):} \\
  The system shall compute a single scalar health score in the range $[0,100]$
  representing the overall calibration quality of the complete camera array.
  For the initial calibration, the health score shall be derived from
  reprojection error statistics.
  For LiFCal-based recalibration, the health score shall be derived from
  LiFCal-specific reprojection and consistency metrics extracted from the combined parameter set.

  \item \textbf{FR5 – Target-Free Online Recalibration (LiFCal-based):} \\
  The system shall execute a LiFCal-based target-free recalibration using automatically prepared focus images and virtual depth data whenever a recalibration cycle is triggered.

  \item \textbf{FR6 – Acceptance and Baseline Update Rule:} \\
  The system shall compare the health score of a newly computed calibration
  against the health score of the currently stored baseline calibration.
  The new calibration shall be accepted and stored as the new baseline
  only if its health score is strictly higher than the baseline score;
  otherwise, the system shall retain the previous baseline calibration.

  \item \textbf{FR7 – Automated Periodic Recalibration Scheduling:} \\
  The system shall support automated, periodic execution of the LiFCal-based recalibration process at a configurable time interval (minimum every 5 minutes).

  \item \textbf{FR8 – Logging and Traceability:} \\
  The system shall log every calibration and recalibration run, including at least timestamps, used image sets, computed health scores, baseline update decisions, and error states.
  
  \item \textbf{FR9 – Abort and Logging on Recalibration Failure:} \\ The system shall abort the current recalibration cycle if a recalibration run fails. In such cases, the system shall retain the currently active baseline calibration and record a descriptive error message, including the failure reason, in the calibration log.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Non-Functional Requirements}
\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{NFR1 – Modularity:} \\
  The system shall be structured into modular components
  (initial calibration, LiFCal recalibration, health monitoring, scheduling, and logging) to support extensibility and maintainability.

  \item \textbf{NFR2 – Reproducibility:} \\
  All calibration results and health evaluations shall be reproducible using stored JSON files and logged metadata.

  \item \textbf{NFR3 – Robustness:} \\
  The calibration and monitoring pipeline shall tolerate moderate variations in scene content and illumination without system failure, even if recalibration quality temporarily degrades.

  \item \textbf{NFR4 – Automation:} \\
  The calibration framework shall operate fully automatically
  after initial setup, without requiring manual intervention
  during periodic monitoring and recalibration.

  \item \textbf{NFR5 – Transparency:} \\
  The system shall provide access to intermediate calibration metrics and logged decision outcomes to allow inspection and analysis of health score computations and acceptance decisions.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}
These requirements form the basis for the system design and implementation presented in the following sections. They were derived following the guidelines and best practices of IEEE Std~830--1998 \cite{720574} and directly support the intended use case of the proposed calibration framework. The functional requirements are reflected in the architectural decisions, UML diagrams, and implementation steps, while the non-functional requirements address relevant quality aspects such as robustness, automation, and maintainability.
\newline

The requirements specification is intentionally kept compact and focused. It is not intended to represent a complete industrial requirements document, but rather a research-oriented specification that is appropriate for the scope and objectives of this bachelor thesis.

\subsubsection{Health Monitoring and Decision Metrics}

As mentioned in the use case description, a key component of the proposed calibration framework is the health monitoring mechanism. This mechanism evaluates the quality of each recalibration attempt and decides whether to accept or reject the new calibration based on defined metrics. To define these metrics, the following formulas are used:

\begin{equation}
\text{HealthScore} = 100 \cdot \left( 1 - \left( 0.6 \cdot E_{\text{reproj}}^{2}
+ 0.4 \cdot E_{\text{rms}}^{2} \right) \right)
\end{equation}
\vspace{0.1cm}


\begin{equation}
E_{\text{reproj}} =
\mathrm{clamp}\!\left(
\frac{e_{\text{reproj}} - R_{\text{BEST}}}
{R_{\text{WORST}} - R_{\text{BEST}}},\; 0,\; 1
\right)
\end{equation}
\vspace{0.1cm}


\begin{equation}
E_{\text{rms}} =
\mathrm{clamp}\!\left(
\frac{e_{\text{rms}} - S_{\text{BEST}}}
{S_{\text{WORST}} - S_{\text{BEST}}},\; 0,\; 1
\right)
\end{equation}
\vspace{0.2cm}


This health score formulation is used for the initial target-based calibration. It combines normalized reprojection error and RMS error into a single scalar score in the range [0,100], where higher values indicate better calibration quality. Clamp functions ensure that the error terms are bounded between 0 and 1 based on predefined best and worst-case thresholds. The weights are chosen to give slightly higher priority to the reprojection error than to the RMS error. This choice follows common practice in the literature, where reprojection error is treated as a primary indicator of calibration correctness and overall geometric accuracy.
\newline

For the LiFCal-based recalibration, a similar health score is computed using LiFCal-specific metrics:

\begin{equation}
\text{HealthScore}_{\text{LiFCal}} =
100 \cdot \left[ 1 -
\left( 0.6 \cdot E_{\text{std}}^{2}
+ 0.4 \cdot E_{\text{mae}}^{2} \right) \right]
\end{equation}
\vspace{0.1cm}


\begin{equation}
E_{\text{std}} =
\mathrm{clamp}\!\left(
\frac{
\sqrt{x_{\text{std}}^{2} + y_{\text{std}}^{2}} - S_{\text{BEST}}
}
{S_{\text{WORST}} - S_{\text{BEST}}},\; 0,\; 1
\right)
\end{equation}
\vspace{0.1cm}


\begin{equation}
E_{\text{mae}} =
\mathrm{clamp}\!\left(
\frac{
\sqrt{x_{\text{mae}}^{2} + y_{\text{mae}}^{2}} - R_{\text{BEST}}
}
{R_{\text{WORST}} - R_{\text{BEST}}},\; 0,\; 1
\right)
\end{equation}
\vspace{0.1cm}


with
\[
R_{\text{BEST}} = 0.03,\quad
R_{\text{WORST}} = 1.0,\quad
S_{\text{BEST}} = 0.0,\quad
S_{\text{WORST}} = 50.0
\]
\vspace{0.05cm}

This formulation is applied to LiFCal-based recalibration results. It evaluates the stability and consistency of the estimated camera parameters using standard deviation and mean absolute error metrics. The resulting health score enables a direct comparison with the baseline calibration to support automated acceptance or rejection decisions. The initial and recalibration health scores are computed differently due to the different result metrics provided by the respective calibration methods. The specific best and worst-case values are defined also based on calibration performance which we discussed in Calibration Parameters and Error Metrics section.

\subsubsection{Runtime Environment and Toolchain} 

Most parts of the implementation are based on established open-source libraries and tools. The primary programming language used is Python, chosen for its extensive ecosystem supporting computer vision, numerical computation, and scientific prototyping. Core libraries include OpenCV for calibration and image processing, NumPy for numerical operations, and Matplotlib for visualization. The LiFCal implementation provided by Fleith et al.~\cite{LiFCal} was integrated into the workflow to perform target-free recalibration.

The development and evaluation were conducted on a system running Ubuntu~20.04.6 LTS. It should be noted that the LiFCal implementation relies on GPU acceleration and requires a compatible NVIDIA GPU to execute the optimization efficiently. Consequently, LiFCal-based recalibration cannot be performed on systems without suitable GPU support.

\section{Discussion of the Results}

In this part of the thesis, the obtained results are analyzed and discussed in detail. The focus is on interpreting the findings from both the initial target-based calibration and the LiFCal-based recalibration. The limitations of the LiFCal method in the given setup are examined, and a comparison between the two calibration approaches is provided. The research questions outlined in the Introduction are also answered based on the results. Finally, potential extensions and improvements for future work are suggested.

\subsection{Interpretation of Initial Calibration Results}

The initial target-based calibration method provided very accurate results in terms of intrinsic and extrinsic parameter estimation. As shown in \autoref{fig:reprojection_error_plot}, the reprojection error remained consistent across all cameras with a value of about 0.03 pixels. This indicates that the calibration process was able to accurately model the camera geometry and lens distortions. The intrinsic parameters, particularly the focal lengths and principal points, also showed small deviations from the ground truth values, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:intrinsic_accuracy}. In this case, the $f_x$ values were very close to the ground truth, while the $f_y$ values exhibited a nearly constant offset of approximately 275 pixels. This systematic deviation is due to the synthetic nature of the data and the specific configuration of the Blender camera model, because the sensor width and height were automatically set. Either the width or the height could have been adjusted and the other parameter would have been calculated accordingly to maintain the aspect ratio. This led to a consistent offset in one of the focal length parameters. 
\newline 

The RMS error values, shown in \autoref{fig:rms_error_plot}, were not as low as expected, ranging from 15 to 35 pixels across the cameras. However, these values are not directly comparable to the per-camera reprojection errors, as the RMS returned by OpenCV's \texttt{stereoCalibrate} represents a joint error over both camera views and also reflects the consistency of the estimated relative camera poses. Small inaccuracies in corner detection or checkerboard poses can therefore result in higher RMS values, especially before applying bundle adjustment.
\newline

The extrinsic parameters also demonstrated good accuracy, with mean translation errors around 0.068 meters and mean rotation errors approximately 0.187 degrees across different image sets, as summarized in \autoref{tab:scenario_summary}. Almost all image sets achieved reprojection errors below 0.04 pixels, indicating a high level of geometric consistency in the estimated camera poses. However, in imageset1 and imageset3, the reprojection errors were slightly higher, around 0.34 pixels and 0.41 pixels, respectively. These two anomalies are caused by the quality of the taken images during the rendering process in Blender. In these two image sets, the checkerboard distance to the camera was larger than in the other image sets, which made it more difficult to detect the corners accurately. This led to less precise calibration results and consequently higher reprojection errors. More poses with closer checkerboard positions would likely have improved the calibration accuracy in these cases. That shows the importance of the variety and quality of the calibration images for achieving optimal results. A higher number of poses does not necessarily guarantee better calibration if the image quality is compromised.
\newline

In \autoref{tab:scenario_summary} it can be observed that the robustness and generalization capabilities of the initial calibration method are stable across different decalibration scenarios. Scenario~A\_Robustness, which tested the same decalibration with varying checkerboard poses, yielded consistent results with low mean translation and rotation errors. Scenario~B\_Generalisation, which involved different decalibrations, also produced reliable calibration outcomes, demonstrating the method's ability to adapt to various camera configurations. This indicates that the initial target-based calibration is effective in maintaining accuracy under different conditions, provided that the calibration images are of sufficient quality. 


\subsection{Limitations of LiFCal in the Given Setup}

The LiFCal-based recalibration results revealed several limitations when applied to the synthetic multi-camera light field setup. The visualization in \autoref{fig:lifcal_reprojection_error_comparison} clearly shows that the reprojection errors after LiFCal-based recalibration are significantly higher than those obtained from the initial target-based calibration. 
\newline

However, it is important to note that LiFCal is designed as a target-free calibration method that relies on feature detection and depth maps. The synthetic data generated in Blender may not fully capture the complexity and variability of real-world scenes, which can negatively affect the performance of LiFCal. In addition, the depth maps used in the LiFCal process are derived from the synthetic scenes and may not accurately represent the depth information that would be obtained from real images.
\newline

A major reason for the high reprojection errors is that LiFCal was primarily developed for micro-lens-array (MLA) light field cameras rather than for multi-camera array systems. The assumptions made by LiFCal regarding the camera model and the image formation process do not fully hold for the multi-camera array setup used in this thesis. This mismatch can lead to inaccuracies in the estimated camera parameters and consequently to higher reprojection errors. Nevertheless, LiFCal provides a systematic target-free recalibration approach, which is a significant advantage in scenarios where the use of physical calibration targets is impractical.
\newline

Furthermore, as discussed in the use case description, the main purpose of the automatic recalibration software is to maintain calibration quality over time without manual intervention. Although LiFCal does not require a calibration target, it still depends on multiple images of the scene captured from different viewpoints. This implies that the camera system must be able to move within the scene in order to generate the required input data for LiFCal. In practical applications, this requirement may not always be feasible, particularly in static environments where the camera system cannot be repositioned easily. Therefore, while LiFCal enables a target-free calibration process, it still relies on a certain degree of controlled motion or data acquisition, which can limit its applicability in fully automated and static scenarios.
\newline

With regard to the first research question (RQ1) formulated in the Introduction—\textit{``Can the target-free calibration method LiFCal be used to automatically calibrate a multi-camera light field system without human interaction or the use of a checkerboard?''}—the results presented in this chapter indicate that LiFCal can be applied in a target-free manner, but its effectiveness in the given multi-camera light field setup is limited. The observed high reprojection errors suggest that LiFCal, in its current form, is not sufficient to achieve accurate calibration for this specific system without further adaptations. Nevertheless, the method provides a valuable foundation for an automated recalibration pipeline that can be improved and extended in future work.


\subsection{Comparison between Initial Calibration and LiFCal}

The comparison between the initial target-based calibration and the LiFCal-based recalibration highlights several key differences in terms of accuracy and limitations. As shown in \autoref{fig:lifcal_reprojection_error_2}, the reprojection errors after LiFCal recalibration are inconsistent across the different runs, while the initial calibration produced stable and low reprojection errors across all cameras and image sets, as illustrated in \autoref{tab:imageset_results}. For the multi-camera array system used in this thesis and the synthetic data generated in Blender, the initial calibration method clearly outperformed LiFCal in terms of accuracy. The health scores summarized in \autoref{tab:calibration_health} further emphasize this discrepancy, with the initial calibration achieving scores above 87, while the highest score obtained with LiFCal was only around 59. This substantial difference indicates that the initial calibration method is more effective at accurately modeling the camera system under the given conditions.
\newline

As mentioned earlier, the extrinsic parameters estimated by LiFCal cannot be directly compared to the ground truth data due to the independent calibration of each camera and the absence of a shared global reference frame. This limitation restricts the evaluation of the overall geometric consistency of the LiFCal results in the same way as for the initial calibration. Consequently, the comparison focuses primarily on reprojection errors and health scores, which provide a more indirect assessment of calibration quality.
\newline

With respect to the second research question (RQ2) posed in the Introduction—\textit{``How well does LiFCal perform when calibrating a multi-camera light field system, and can it replace a classical checkerboard-based calibration?''}—the findings show that LiFCal, in the current setup and within the design of the proposed software, does not perform well enough to replace a classical checkerboard-based calibration. The significantly higher reprojection errors and lower health scores indicate that LiFCal is currently unable to achieve the same level of accuracy as the initial calibration method. In addition, the observed outliers and inconsistencies in the LiFCal results suggest that further refinements and adaptations are necessary to improve its performance for multi-camera light field systems. Therefore, while LiFCal represents a promising approach, it cannot currently serve as a complete replacement for traditional calibration methods in this context.


\subsection{Comparison to the State of the Art}

The state of the art in multi-camera light field calibration is still dominated by target-based approaches that use physical calibration patterns (e.g., checkerboards) to estimate intrinsic and extrinsic parameters reliably. The initial calibration implemented in this thesis follows this classical line of work and achieved consistently low reprojection errors (around $0.03$px across cameras) as well as high health scores (roughly in the range of $87$ to $98$ across the evaluated image sets). Overall, these results are in line with what is typically reported for well-controlled target-based calibration in multi-camera systems.
\newline

Target-free approaches such as LiFCal are attractive because they avoid physical targets and, in principle, allow calibration updates with less manual setup. In the setup used in this thesis, LiFCal did not reach the same level of accuracy, as the reprojection errors were clearly higher across cameras, and the resulting health scores stayed far below those of the initial calibration (around $52$ to $59$ in the three runs). This suggests that, at least in the current form and for the considered multi-camera array scenario, LiFCal cannot replace the checkerboard-based baseline.
\newline

A practical contribution of this thesis to the state of the art is the automated calibration pipeline. It combines an initial reference calibration with periodic target-free recalibration attempts and a decision mechanism based on a health score. This directly addresses the real-world problem of calibration drift over time, where repeated manual target-based calibration can become impractical. Using Blender-based synthetic scenes also made it possible to run controlled experiments with access to ground truth parameters, which are difficult to obtain in physical multi-camera setups. This innovative approach provides a useful method for evaluating and comparing calibration techniques in a reproducible manner.
\newline

In summary, the initial target-based calibration matches established practice and produces strong results in this environment, while the LiFCal-based recalibration mainly shows potential but requires further adaptation before it can be considered a robust alternative for multi-camera light field systems. 


\subsection{Potential Extensions and Improvements}

The current state of the implemented calibration software provides a solid foundation. However, there are several potential extensions and improvements that could be explored in future work to fulfill the goal of fully automated and reliable multi-camera light field calibration. One promising direction is to adapt and enhance the LiFCal method specifically for multi-camera array systems. This could involve modifying the underlying camera model assumptions and optimization strategies to better suit the geometry and characteristics of multi-camera setups.
\newline

An optional improvement would be to implement the software in a more performance-oriented programming language, such as C++ or Rust. This would allow faster execution of the calibration pipeline, which is particularly important for real-time applications or scenarios where frequent recalibration is necessary. Additionally, integrating parallel processing techniques could further speed up the calibration process, especially when dealing with large image sets or high-resolution images.
\newline

The evaluation of the calibration framework using a real multi-camera light field system would be a crucial next step. This would provide a more realistic assessment of the calibration methods and their performance in practical scenarios. Testing the LiFCal method on real-world data would also help to identify additional challenges or limitations that may not be apparent in synthetic environments.
\newline

In order to explicitly answer the last research question (RQ3) from the Introduction—\textit{``Under which conditions and requirements can an automatic, target-free recalibration of a multi-camera light field system be successfully performed?''}—the following conditions and requirements can be identified based on the findings of this thesis:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{LiFCal Adaptation:} The LiFCal method needs to be specifically adapted for multi-camera array systems to account for their unique geometry and characteristics.
    \item \textbf{Scene Complexity:} The scenes used for target-free recalibration should contain sufficient texture and depth variation to enable robust feature detection and depth estimation.
    \item \textbf{Camera Movement:} The camera system must be capable of capturing multiple viewpoints of the scene to provide the necessary data for LiFCal.
    \item \textbf{Quality of Input Data:} High-quality images with minimal noise and distortion are essential for accurate calibration results.
    \item \textbf{Computational Efficiency:} The calibration software should be optimized for performance to allow timely recalibration in practical applications.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation with Real Data:} Testing and validating the calibration methods using real multi-camera light field systems is crucial to ensure their effectiveness in practical scenarios.
    \item \textbf{Health Monitoring:} Implementing a robust health monitoring mechanism to assess calibration quality and decide when recalibration is necessary.
\end{itemize}

In conclusion, the requirements outlined in the Methods and Materials section and the potential improvements discussed here provide a guideline for future research and development in the field of data-driven automatic multi-camera light field calibration. Addressing these aspects will be key to achieving reliable and efficient target-free recalibration in practical applications.

\section{Conclusion and Outlook}

The final chapter summarizes the key aspects of the thesis. On one hand, the main achievements and findings are recapped in the Conclusion subsection. On the other hand, in the Outlook subsection, potential future directions and improvements for the calibration software and methods are discussed.

\subsection{Conclusion}

In this thesis, a data-driven automatic calibration software for multi-camera light field systems was implemented and evaluated. It combines an initial target-based calibration using a checkerboard, which is used to estimate the calibration parameters as a reference, with a target-free recalibration approach based on the LiFCal method. In addition, a health score monitoring mechanism is used to decide whether a new calibration result improves the overall calibration quality. The health score ranges from 0 to 100, where higher values correspond to better calibration quality. The system is designed to perform recalibration automatically, without requiring manual intervention, in order to counteract calibration drift over time. The software was implemented in Python, and synthetic image data generated with Blender was used to evaluate the calibration results against known ground truth parameters.
\newline

The results showed that the initial target-based calibration achieved high accuracy, with reprojection errors of approximately $0.03$px per camera and health scores ranging between about $87$ and $98$ across the evaluated image sets. In contrast, the LiFCal-based recalibration resulted in significantly higher reprojection errors for all cameras and lower health scores, which ranged between approximately $52$ and $59$ in the performed recalibration runs. These results indicate that, in the given multi-camera light field setup, LiFCal is currently not suitable to replace a classical checkerboard-based calibration. The main limitations of LiFCal in this context were analyzed, and possible reasons for its reduced performance were discussed.
\newline

In conclusion, the implemented software provides a useful framework for automatic multi-camera light field calibration and systematic evaluation of calibration quality. However, further adaptations and improvements are required before the target-free recalibration approach can achieve a calibration accuracy comparable to that of the initial target-based method.

\subsection{Outlook}

For future work, several extensions of the calibration software were identified. One possible direction is to adapt the LiFCal method more explicitly to multi-camera array systems. This would require changes to the underlying camera model and the optimization process so they match the geometry and constraints of camera arrays more closely. Such adaptations may lead to more stable results when the method is applied to multi-camera configurations. 
\newline

Another potential improvement concerns the implementation itself. Rewriting the software in a faster programming language, such as C++, would reduce runtime and make repeated recalibration more practical. This becomes relevant in real-world scenarios where recalibration is performed frequently or where time constraints play a role. 
\newline

A further step would be the evaluation of the framework on a real multi-camera light field system. Testing on real hardware would allow a more realistic assessment of calibration accuracy and robustness. It would also expose practical issues which do not appear in synthetic experiments, such as noise, mechanical inaccuracies, or synchronization effects. Overall, the project presented in this thesis provides a solid foundation for further research and development in the field of multi-camera light field calibration.
